# Wisconsin Opportunity — Decision Brief

> [!abstract] 🟡 TL;DR
> Ava's dad floated a job in Wisconsin with Tyler's businesses (Ultimate Insulation, P1, others).
> The instinct to **test it for 1–3 months instead of deciding forever** is the right one — it's the
> only version of this that doesn't require an irreversible move. But the trial only works if three
> numbers get pinned down first: the wage floor, the real liquidation value of the cars, and a
> written operator deal for OTL Rental. Don't talk pay before knowing the floor.

---

## 🧭 Context

As of 2026-09-14 this is an informal, verbal opening — "if you ever wanted to make a change, there'd
be a place for you." Not a written offer, no role named, no number attached. Everything below treats
it as a **lead worth qualifying**, not a job in hand.

The knot is that it collides with three things at once:

1. **The cars.** OTL Rental is the current income — roughly **$6,000/month** paid to the owner seat.
2. **The debts.** Money owed to **Jim Bob** (startup capital) and a payout owed to **Thomas**.
3. **The house.** A mortgage in Richmond Hill that keeps billing whether or not anyone lives there.

---

## 🔀 The most important move: split this into two decisions

> [!tip] 🟣 These are separate, and letting one force the other is the trap
> **Decision A — do I want to be in Wisconsin doing this work?**
> **Decision B — do I liquidate the car business?**
>
> "If I sold absolutely everything I'd have nothing left" is the answer to **B**. It is not a
> requirement for **A**. Decision A can be tested for three months for the price of a reduced
> paycheck. Decision B is permanent, and once the fleet is gone there's no getting back in at the
> same cost basis.
>
> **Test A first. Let what's learned there decide B.** Making B now, blind, to enable A is backwards.

There's also a sequencing bonus that's easy to miss: if Wisconsin works out and the fleet does get
sold later, that sale happens **deliberately over 6–12 months from a position of having income** —
retail prices, one car at a time — instead of as a fire-sale to fund a move. That difference is real
money, and it's a straight argument for trial-run-first.

## 💸 The trial-run cash math

The structure from the brainstorm, laid out. The $6,000/month that currently goes to the owner seat
gets re-split:

| Line | Today | Trial v1 — keep $1k | Trial v2 — all to debt |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| OTL operating budget for the operator seat | $6,000 | $6,000 | $6,000 |
| → stand-in operator | $0 | −$4,000 | −$4,000 |
| → to you personally | **$6,000** | **$1,000** | **$0** |
| → to Jim Bob / Thomas paydown | $0 | **$1,000** | **$2,000** |
| OTL → Nicholas Jaime Private Banking (loan repayment) | continues | continues | continues |
| Wisconsin wage | — | **TBD** | **TBD** |

> [!danger] 🔴 The gap that decides everything
> Personal income during the trial is **Wisconsin net pay + $1,000** (v1) or **+ $0** (v2).
> That has to carry *both* the Richmond Hill mortgage *and* living in Wisconsin at the same time —
> two housing costs, one income. $1,000/month from OTL does not close that. **The Wisconsin wage is
> the only variable that can.** That's why the number comes before the conversation, not after.

### Lean v1 for the trial, not v2

Taking the $1,000 is the better call for a 1–3 month test even though v2 pays down debt faster.
Running two households with zero personal buffer is how a trial ends early for reasons that have
nothing to do with whether the job was good. Shift to v2 after the trial, once the Wisconsin income
is proven and only one household is being carried.

> [!warning] 🟠 One thing to verify, not assume
> **Nicholas Jaime Private Banking** — if that entity is effectively you, the loan repayment is money
> landing on your own side of the ledger and belongs in the personal-income math above. If it's
> earmarked or committed elsewhere, it isn't spendable and shouldn't be counted. Confirm which
> before treating it as part of the cushion.

## 🎯 The floor number to walk in with

Before any pay conversation, compute this. It's one sitting with the bank app:

```
Wisconsin net pay must cover:

    Richmond Hill carrying cost   (mortgage + escrow + insurance + utilities on an empty house)
  + Wisconsin living cost         (rent or room + food + fuel + the drive home now and then)
  + personal minimum              (phone, insurance, any personal car note, groceries)
  + buffer                        (~15% — there is always something)
  − $1,000                        (the OTL draw under trial v1)
  ─────────────────────────────
  = NET monthly floor
```

Then gross it up before naming a number out loud: **gross ≈ net ÷ 0.75** as a rough W-2 estimate.
A $4,000/month net floor is roughly a **$5,300/month gross**, or about **$32/hour at 40 hours**.
Those conversions are rough — the point is not to quote a gross number that quietly nets 25% less
than what's needed.

> [!tip] 🟣 Walk in with two numbers, not one
> The **floor** (below this the trial can't physically work) and the **target** (what makes it
> clearly worth doing). Ask what the role pays before naming either. If the offer is a range, the
> floor decides whether there's anything to discuss.

## 🧮 The liquidation question — honestly

"I'd sell everything, pay everybody off, and have nothing left" is a genuinely important statement,
because it means **equity in the business is roughly zero**. If that's accurate, then:

- **Selling** yields about $0 in cash — but it buys a **clean slate**: no Jim Bob, no Thomas, no
  fleet to worry about from 1,000 miles away. That freedom is worth something real, especially if
  the debt is what's actually generating the stress.
- **Delegating** yields **$2,000/month** in retained cash plus the loan repayment stream plus the
  option to come back to it — but the obligations to Jim Bob and Thomas stay, and so does the risk
  of a fleet being run by someone else.

Neither is obviously right. What *is* obviously right: **that "roughly zero" is currently a feeling,
not a figure**, and it's driving the biggest decision on the table. One afternoon fixes that:

```
  total realistic fleet value   (per-unit, what each would actually sell for — not clean retail)
− payoffs / liens on the units
− owed to Jim Bob
− owed to Thomas
─────────────────────────────
= actual net position
```

If that comes back at $40k instead of $0, this is a different conversation entirely. If it comes
back negative, that's worth knowing before making plans around it.

> [!warning] 🟠 Needs content — the numbers this note is missing
> Amount owed to Jim Bob · amount owed to Thomas · fleet count and realistic per-unit value ·
> Richmond Hill monthly carrying cost · OTL → Private Banking repayment amount. Fill these in and
> most of the above stops being a framework and starts being an answer.

## 🟠 Risks worth naming out loud

**The operator.** Someone paid $4,000/month to run a fleet he doesn't own, while the owner is a
thousand miles away. The failure mode isn't theft — it's quiet degradation: deferred maintenance,
soft collections, a damage claim handled badly, a customer relationship lost. It shows up late.
Mitigations belong in writing before leaving, not after.

**$4,000 doesn't buy the whole job.** The owner seat isn't only the operator seat — bookkeeping,
insurance, the Jim Bob and Thomas relationships, buying and selling units, the judgment calls. That
part doesn't hand off. **Plan on still working 5–10 hours a week on OTL from Wisconsin**, and say so
to the Wisconsin side up front so it isn't a surprise.

**Jim Bob and Thomas hearing it secondhand.** "He moved to Wisconsin" reads as *he's leaving* if it
arrives from anyone but you. Get ahead of it, and lead with the fact that the payments are
*continuing and increasing*. Under trial v1 they're getting $1,000/month they weren't getting
before — that's the headline, and it's a true one.

**The offer may be softer than it sounded.** "Basically sounds like a job" is not a job. Before any
of this gets built on, it has to become: a role, a rate, a start date, and a person who has the
authority to say those things.

**Two households, one income.** The single largest failure risk in the whole plan, and the reason
the wage floor matters more than anything else here.

## ❓ What to ask — the Wisconsin conversation

Best framed as curiosity, not negotiation. "You mentioned there'd be a spot — can I ask what that
would actually look like?"

- Which business, and what's the actual day-to-day? (Ultimate Insulation vs. P1 vs. something else)
- What does the role pay — hourly or salary, and what's the range?
- W-2 or 1099? *(1099 means self-employment tax and no withholding — it changes the floor math a lot)*
- Is there health insurance, and when does it start?
- Housing — is there a place to stay, a per diem, or is that entirely on me?
- **Would a 1–3 month trial work for you?** Framed honestly: there's a business and a house to
  unwind at home, and coming up to work a season is the way to do this right by both sides.
- If it works, what does it grow into? A role, a percentage, a territory — is there a ceiling?
- Is there a timing need on your end, or is this open-ended?

> [!tip] 🟣 Say the trial part out loud
> A trial that's secretly a trial is worse than one that's stated. Naming it sets expectations on
> both sides, makes leaving-if-it-doesn't-fit a non-event instead of a burned bridge, and reads as
> someone being straight about their obligations — which is the good version of this.
